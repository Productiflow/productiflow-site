Why "one request at a time" produces better eLearning

The agency model bundles everything into a single bloated project. Here's why a focused queue delivers faster — and better.

Most eLearning agencies pitch you a statement of work with twelve modules, a six-month timeline, and a discovery phase that takes longer than some companies' entire training backlogs. By the time the first draft lands, the stakeholders have changed, the product has shipped three updates, and the onboarding flow you were documenting looks nothing like what's in production.

The one-request model isn't a constraint — it's a design decision. Here's how it changes everything.

Scope creep starts at the proposal

When you scope ten modules at once, you're making assumptions about all ten. Which personas matter for module six? What's the ideal assessment format for module nine? Nobody knows yet — but the proposal commits to it anyway, because that's how agencies close deals.

A single-request queue forces specificity. You can't submit a vague request; you have to tell us the audience, the behavior you're changing, the context in which they'll take the course. That brief is where most eLearning projects actually succeed or fail — not in development.

Feedback loops collapse

With a twelve-module project, review cycles are monthly at best. A single module moves through a tight loop: brief → draft → review → final → delivered. The whole cycle can happen in under a week. You stay close to the work. You catch problems before they compound.

When your team changes something — a process, a product, a policy — you can update the affected module without touching nine others. Modularity is maintainability.

Quality is a function of focus

When a designer is context-switching between eight client projects, quality suffers at the margins. A focused queue means each request gets a designer's full attention — not split focus across a sprawling SOW.

This is especially true for brand fidelity. Getting your colors, typography, and voice right in module one means module two starts from a solid foundation. Iterating across one module at a time lets the work get better, not just bigger.

The right pace for most L&D teams

Most internal L&D teams don't need twelve modules simultaneously. They need the next thing — the onboarding refresh, the compliance update, the new hire orientation — done well and done quickly. A subscription model matches that reality. Subscribe when you need capacity, pause when you don't.

That's not a workaround. It's just how knowledge work actually flows.
